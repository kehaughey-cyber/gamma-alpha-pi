# The Story

## How It Started

I started my life in construction as a laborer on a jackhammer. Not management. Not oversight. A jackhammer. I worked large projects, the World War Memorial in Indianapolis, prisons, military bases, schools. I worked hard and I worked smart, and over time I worked my way up to superintendent. I ran a library parking garage. I ran an air traffic control tower in Fort Wayne.

When we topped out on that tower, I got laid off. It was not the first time. In construction, layoffs come with the seasons and with the economy and sometimes with no warning at all. But this time something shifted in me. I decided I was done waiting for someone else to hand me the next job. I started my own company.

It grew fast. We were traveling the country building megachurches and restaurants. Along the way I met a man named Van Hughes. Van is one of the most brilliant people I have encountered in my life. We worked together on something ahead of its time, building what would become a prototype for smartphones and small cell networks. We used a handheld computer called an OQO as a platform, built wireless nodes on fiber optic backbones, and created small cell networks before that was a common concept. Van and I talked about a lot of things beyond the work. Philosophy. Theology. Economics. The structure of systems and how they fail.

## Watching the Train Wreck

In 2006, Van and I identified a structural problem in the economy. Short selling. The mortgage crisis. A concurrent bubble. We could see it. We talked about it. And then we watched it unfold in slow motion, which is one of the more helpless feelings a man can have. Knowing something is coming and being unable to stop it.

The financial crisis began unwinding in the fall of 2007. I was mid-project when banks started calling to tell me they were no longer funding the work. Not just future draws. Draws I had already submitted invoices for. Money I had already spent on materials and labor. Gone. Just gone.

I watched good men around me lose everything. Strong men. Men who had built businesses and livelihoods over decades, some over generations. Friends. Colleagues. The whole ecosystem I had built my life inside of. And I was not just watching.

I was losing everything too.

My business collapsed. My equipment, gone. My crew, gone. The men I was responsible for, gone. I was still on the road trying to hold whatever remained together when my wife called to tell me the sheriff had come to serve the eviction notice. I was still traveling when she called again to tell me they had come to repossess her car. I lost my home. I went through bankruptcy. Foreclosure. Repossession. All of it.

The only things that stayed were my wife and my children.

I was not watching the world burn from a safe distance. I was in the fire. And I kept moving anyway.

## The Hallway

It was Van who gave me the frame for what we were living through. He told me that life is like a hallway. Sometimes it is lit. Sometimes it is dark. And when it is dark, it is like walking with an extension cord on a light. The cord gets you down the hall. It illuminates what is right in front of you. But eventually, you run out of cord. You reach the end of it.

And when you reach the end of the cord, you have a choice.

You can stop. You can stand there in the dark at the end of your light and decide that because you cannot see what is ahead, there is nothing worth moving toward.

Or you can unplug. You can leave the light behind and keep going into the dark, trusting that you are part of something greater than what you can currently see, trusting that your purpose is real and that it matters, trusting that the hallway continues even when you cannot prove it.

That is faith. Not a feeling. Not a comfort. A decision. The decision to unplug and keep going when you have every rational reason to stop.

Faith is not the absence of darkness. It is the decision to keep moving through it.

## What It Cost and What It Was Worth

I kept going. Through the bankruptcy and the foreclosure and the repossessions and the shame and the grief of watching men I cared about suffer losses I could not undo. I kept going when I had no business still standing, no home to return to, no equipment, no crew. I kept going because Van was right, and somewhere in me I knew it.

It was worth it.

Not because everything came back easily or quickly. Not because the losses stopped hurting. But because a man who unplugs and keeps going discovers something about himself that a man who stops at the end of his cord never finds. He discovers that the hallway continues. That there is more ahead. That the darkness was not the end of the story. It was the middle of it.

WHAT THIS STORY TEACHES ABOUT VOICE

Notice how this story moves. It starts with the concrete and specific: a jackhammer, a job site, a company, a named man, a named crisis. It earns its way to the philosophical. The metaphor of the extension cord does not arrive until the reader understands what the darkness actually cost. That is the sequence. Ground the reader in the real. Then, and only then, lift him toward the meaning.

The voice does not perform suffering. It states it plainly and moves on. 'The only things that stayed were my wife and my children.' That sentence does not ask for sympathy. It simply tells the truth, and the truth carries its own weight.

The voice does not arrive at hope cheaply. It earns it. 'It was worth it.' Three words. After everything that preceded them, those three words land like a stone.

Write with this economy. Trust the weight of what is real. Do not decorate it. Do not soften it. Do not reach for the meaning before the ground is ready to hold it.
